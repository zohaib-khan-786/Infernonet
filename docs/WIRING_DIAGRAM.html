FreshGuard — Complete Wiring & Pinout Diagram

ESP8266MOD cold-storage food-safety monitor · derived from the compiled firmware (FreshGuard.ino) so the wiring and the code cannot disagree.

Before you power anything up

  • The ESP8266 is 3.3 V only. Nothing 5 V may touch a GPIO. Your BSS138 level shifter is what makes the 5 V LCD safe.
  • You do not yet have a PCF8574. It is not an accessory — without it the firmware reports Sensor Fault permanently and no food verdict is ever produced.
  • Common ground is mandatory. USB negative, every module GND, and the shifter's GND terminal must all be tied.

1System topology

Three separate buses leave the ESP8266: a 3.3 V I²C bus, a 5 V-shifted I²C branch for the LCD, and hardware SPI for the RFID reader. Everything switched (LEDs, buzzer) hangs off the I/O expander, never off the MCU.

Why there is no GPIO left

Seven pins serve the three buses, and the two remaining candidates (GPIO0, GPIO2) are boot-strapped — pulling either one at power-up puts the ESP8266 into the wrong boot mode. That is the entire reason the design moves the reed switch, LEDs, buzzer and RC522 reset onto a PCF8574 expander rather than onto the MCU.

4PCF8574 channel map

P0 is the only input; P1–P5 are outputs. The configuration register is written as 0xC1, which makes P0, P6 and P7 inputs and P1–P5 outputs.

Why one MOSFET, and why not an NPN or a ULN2003A

The buzzer is the only load that needs a transistor, because it is the only one that is both inductive and over 25 mA.

MOSFET, not NPN: the port's 100 µA HIGH is ample to charge a MOSFET gate (a few picofarads, finished in well under a microsecond, and drawing nothing thereafter) but nowhere near enough to hold an NPN base in saturation. An NPN here would give a click, not a beep.

TO-220, not TO-92: purely mechanical. TO-92 leads are 0.65 mm apart and line up with no perfboard hole spacing whatsoever, so it cannot be inserted without bending. TO-220 leads are 2.54 mm and drop straight into three adjacent holes. A ULN2003A was considered and rejected: it inverts the logic, needs four extra pull-up resistors because its Darlington inputs draw more than 100 µA, and its inputs cannot be held off at boot, so the buzzer would sound on every power cycle.

Why the LEDs need no transistor, and the buzzer does

The LEDs are driven from the rail into the pin, so the pin's strong pull-down MOSFET closes the path at about 8 mA — inside the 25 mA sink rating. In the off state the weak 100 µA source simply holds the LED off at a few tens of millivolts. No driver transistor is involved and the pin never sources more than 100 µA.

The buzzer is different twice over. It is inductive and draws up to 30 mA, which exceeds the 25 mA sink rating — and switching an inductive load without a flyback path spikes back into the shared 5 V rail and the I²C bus. Hence the one MOSFET and the 1N4148.

A MOSFET rather than an NPN, because the port's 100 µA is ample to charge a gate (a few picofarads, done in under a microsecond) but nowhere near enough to hold an NPN base in saturation. The same 100 µA is precisely what P5's TTL input needs, which is why the RC522 reset goes straight in — that is the port's intended use.

The one part substitution that will silently fail

Use IRFZ44N or IRLZ44N — TO-220, logic-level, Vgs(th) 1–2 V, so the 3.3 V HIGH turns it fully on. Do not fit an IRF540N. It is the most common MOSFET in the world and its Vgs(th) is specified at 2–4 V, which means at a 3.3 V gate it is not guaranteed to conduct at all. The symptom is a buzzer that appears completely dead.

Three leads at 2.54 mm pitch drop straight into three adjacent perfboard holes. That is the reason for the TO-220 package: TO-92 leads are 0.65 mm apart and align with no perfboard hole spacing at all.

What the firmware now does about preheat drift

The datasheet asks for 24–48 hours of preheat before the element settles. The firmware warms up for 60 seconds — long enough to stop a cold sensor raising a false alarm, far short of a settled resistance. On its own that meant the remaining drift accumulated as a rising delta over a shift and could read as spoilage that was not there.

The firmware therefore tracks the baseline slowly (2% per averaging pass, a ~4 minute time constant) but only while the measured delta is already inside the clear threshold. That gate is what makes it safe: tracking can never walk the baseline up into a real event, because a genuine gas rise outpaces it and stops tracking on the next pass. It is RAM-only by design — persisting a tracked baseline would mean thousands of LittleFS writes a year for no benefit, since the baseline is re-captured on every cold boot.

6Power distribution

5 V rail — from USB / VIN

  • LCD backpack VCC
  • Buzzer positive terminal
  • LED anodes (via series resistor)
  • Level shifter HV terminal
  • MQ-135 VCC — verify its supply requirement first

3.3 V rail — board regulator output

  • ESP8266 (its own onboard regulator)
  • BMP280, DS3231, ADS1115, PCF8574
  • RC522
  • I²C pull-ups, reed pull-up, gate pull-downs
  • Level shifter LV terminal

Never join the two rails

5 V and 3.3 V must meet only inside the level shifter. A single stray jumper between the LCD's VCC and an ESP8266 pin destroys the module. The shifter is the only bridge between the domains.

Decoupling: 100 nF ceramic at each module's VCC/GND, right at the pins, plus 470 µF bulk on the 5 V rail. The ESP8266's Wi-Fi bursts draw well over 300 mA — if the board's regulator or the USB lead is marginal, brownouts will look like random sensor faults.

7Pre-power safety checklist

Blocking items

  • PCF8574 fitted and addressed 0x20. Without it the firmware latches Sensor Fault forever. Check it is the PCF8574, not the PCF8574A (0x38).
  • DS3231 charging path. The HW-084 board charges its cell. On a non-rechargeable CR2032 that is a safety hazard — remove the charging resistor or fit a rechargeable cell. Verify which you have.
  • Level shifter orientation. LV to 3V3, HV to 5V, GND to common ground. LV/HV reversed destroys the translator.

Verify before relying on it

  • MQ-135 divider. A0 → 4.7 kΩ → ADS1115 AIN0 → 2.2 kΩ → GND. This keeps AIN0 under 3300 mV for any input up to ~10.35 V at A0. The resistor values are placeholders until measured.
  • Reed switch polarity. Closed must read LOW. If your switch is normally-closed, invert the REED_CLOSED_LEVEL constant and re-test.
  • Inductive reed coil. If yours has a coil rather than bare contacts, add a flyback diode with its cathode to the P0 side, anode to GND. Confirm polarity before soldering.
  • Buzzer type — resolved. Use a TMB12A05 (5 V active buzzer, 12 mm, ~30 mA, 2300 Hz, built-in oscillator). A TMB12A12 is a 12 V part rated 8–15 V and will be silent at 5 V; using it would require a second power supply on a USB-powered device. Because the buzzer is active, plain on/off from the expander is correct and no tone generator is needed in firmware.

Pull-up resistors: check before you fit any

I²C pull-ups are almost certainly already on your modules. The Adafruit BMP280 and ADS1115, most PCF8574 and DS3231 breakouts all carry 4.7–10 kΩ on SDA and SCL, usually with a solder bridge to remove them. The BSS138 shifter board carries its own for the LCD side. Power off and measure from SDA to 3V3: if you read roughly 4.7–10 kΩ, they are present and you do not need to fit any. Do not add a second set on top — four modules in parallel gives ~1.2 kΩ, which is acceptable but worth knowing.

No DHT11. It has been removed: the BME280 measures humidity itself. The 10 kΩ reed pull-up is the only 10 kΩ in the build.

The reed switch pull-up is the one judgement call. PCF8574 input ports have an internal pull-up of roughly 33–50 kΩ, so P0 will read HIGH with the switch open and no external resistor at all. It will work. For a bare switch on a cable sitting next to a fridge compressor, 10 kΩ is cheap insurance against noise, which is why it is listed as recommended rather than required.

Why capacitors cannot be delegated to the modules

A resistor is a static property — you can point a meter at it and see whether it is there. That is why pull-ups are usually already on your modules. A capacitor and a flyback diode protect against transients, and by definition you cannot confirm either with a meter before it matters.

Decoupling. The disturbance is created by the inductance of the wire connecting the module. Even 10 cm of hookup wire is roughly 50–100 nH, and a fast current edge across that inductance produces a voltage spike at the far end, at the pin. A cap soldered onto the module is at the wrong end of the wire to respond to it.

Bulk on the 5 V rail. This belongs to the shared supply path, not to any module. Three loads fight over one USB feed: ESP8266 Wi-Fi transients above 300 mA, the MQ-135 heater at ~150 mA, and the LCD backlight plus buzzer. No sensor can buffer a transient happening at the far end of the USB cable.

Only three decoupling caps are listed, not one per module. The low-current sensors draw so little that their own decoupling barely matters. The ones that count are the ESP8266 rail, the MQ-135 supply, and the 5 V bulk.

The one part not to economise on: the 1N4148

A buzzer is an inductive load. Switching it off makes the collapsing magnetic field generate a spike of V = L·di/dt that can reach tens of volts on a 5 V rail. That spike is created by the act of switching, which happens outside the buzzer — no module can contain it, because the module has no idea when you switch it. Every buzzer datasheet assumes you will flyback it.

Without the diode the spike lands across the MOSFET drain and couples back through the shared ground and 5 V rails into every other module. In practice: sensor faults that make no sense, MCU brownouts, and eventually a dead MOSFET. On a device meant to sit unattended in a food bank, that is a reliability landmine for about two cents.

Exception: a bare glass reed switch is just two contacts and stores no energy, so it needs no diode. Only an inductive reed coil does.

Three part substitutions that will silently break the build

  • BME280, not BMP280 — confirmed. These modules are routinely mislabelled in both directions, and the two are not interchangeable. The firmware uses the Adafruit_BME280 driver, whose begin() accepts chip id 0x60 only. A genuine BMP280 returns 0x58 and would be rejected. The two share the temperature and pressure register map, so swapping drivers to suit the part actually fitted is the correct fix, not a workaround.
  • PCF8574 ≠ PCF8574A. The "A" variant answers on 0x38. The firmware looks only at 0x20, so the A version is invisible and the device latches Sensor Fault forever.
  • RC522 is 3.3 V only. It is the module most often destroyed by a 5 V miswire. It is the one item in this build where giving it the "correct" 5 V rail will brick it.

Humidity now comes from the BME280. The DHT11 has been removed, so the BME280 is the only environmental sensor: temperature, pressure and humidity all come from chip id 0x60, and one failure takes all three down. This also frees GPIO16. The one thing to know is that Adafruit_BME280 has an empty constructor, so humidity oversampling reads NAN until setSampling() is called explicitly — the firmware does this at boot, because the alternative is a silent permanent humidity fault.
